Why single cell

A recent project took me into “virtual cell” models: learned simulators meant to answer questions such as what happens to this cell type if I knock down this gene? without running the experiment. I came to the field from machine learning and applied mathematics, not biology, and the first thing I met was a paradox.

Single-cell biology has done what deep learning usually asks for. CZ CELLxGENE aggregates on the order of a hundred million cells (CZI Cell Science Program et al. 2025), chemical screens such as Tahoe-100M add another hundred million (Zhang et al. 2025), and foundation models such as Geneformer and scGPT were pretrained on tens of millions of transcriptomes (Theodoris et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2024). Yet in 2025, a careful evaluation showed that these models, and the specialised deep perturbation models, did not outperform simple linear baselines at predicting the effect of a genetic perturbation, on the tasks tested (Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025). Other groups found simple controls and representations hard to beat as well (Wong et al. 2025; Bendidi et al. 2024), and a lively debate followed about whether the models are weak or the metrics are blind (Miller et al. 2026; Mejia et al. 2025; Viñas Torné et al. 2025).

I wanted to understand that paradox properly, which meant learning the field from the measurement up. This post gathers what came out of that effort, in three parts: a course (Section 2), a small study testing one specific question (Section 3), and an attempt to answer the larger question of whether scale will eventually win here as it did elsewhere (Section 4).

A course, in French and English

The course was written largely with an AI assistant, under my direction and with my review. I am sharing it because it may help others coming to the field from machine learning. It exists in French and English.

The course is organised around one target that every model in the field approximates in some way,

\[ p\big(X \mid \mathrm{do}(a),\, c,\, d,\, t\big), \]

the distribution of a cell’s count profile \(X\) when we intervene to set \(a\) (the \(\mathrm{do}\) operator of causal inference, as opposed to merely observing it), in a context \(c\) (cell type, donor, cell line), at dose \(d\) and time \(t\). Three levels of capability are often conflated under “cell model”: representing observed states, predicting the response to an intervention, and deciding which experiment to run next. Each level presupposes the previous one but does not follow from it. A good atlas embedding says nothing in general about interventions.

The twelve chapters follow that ladder:

Theme Chapters
Foundations 1. From the atlas to the virtual cell · 2. Biology and measurement, from DNA to the UMI count · 3. Count statistics and the analysis pipeline
Representing 4. Latent representation models (PCA, scVI and relatives) · 5. Foundation models and tokenisation
Predicting 6. Perturbation data · 7. Perturbation prediction (scGen, CPA, GEARS, optimal transport, flow matching) · 8. Time and dynamics · 9. Cell imaging and the image–transcriptome link
Deciding 10. Evaluating a perturbation model · 11. Closed loop: active learning and experimental design · 12. Open questions and perspectives

Three appendices give a machine learning refresher, answers to the self-assessment questions, and a glossary. The representation, foundation-model and prediction chapters end by placing their models along the same four choices: the likelihood (Gaussian on log counts, negative binomial, categorical on tokens, none), the working space (genes, latent, tokens, image features), the conditioning (one-hot, gene embedding, knowledge graph), and how control and treated cells are coupled when the measurement destroys the cell (mean shift, latent translation, optimal transport, flow). Reading the literature through those four choices makes the field far less disorderly than it first looks.

A small study: does the benchmark choose good experiments?

The question

Most of the debate on perturbation benchmarks is about calibration: does a metric separate an informative predictor from a trivial one, such as predicting the average effect for every gene? That matters, and recent work has made real progress on it: with well-calibrated metrics, deep perturbation models can beat uninformative baselines (Miller et al. 2026; see also Mejia et al. 2025). But a benchmark has a second role that calibration does not address.

Picture a lab with budget for 20 experiments among 1,000 candidate perturbations. It trains several models that predict the effect of each perturbation, keeps the one with the best validation score, and runs the 20 perturbations that this model ranks as strongest. The benchmark metric has quietly become part of a decision policy: it decides which experiments get run. Nothing guarantees that the model with the lowest prediction error is the one that picks the best experiments.

So the study asks: when a predictor is chosen with a usual metric, how many of the truly strongest perturbations does it recover, compared with choosing it by that recovery rate directly? The unit of the answer is hits lost.

Setting

The data are the two genome-scale CRISPRi Perturb-seq screens of essential genes from Replogle et al. (2022), in the harmonised scPerturb release (Peidli et al. 2024). In a Perturb-seq screen, each cell receives a guide RNA that reduces the expression of one gene (a knockdown, here by CRISPR interference, CRISPRi), and both the guide and the cell’s transcriptome, the expression level of every gene, are read. Both screens target essential genes, those a cell needs to survive or divide. K562, a leukaemia line, is the discovery dataset; RPE1, a non-cancerous epithelial line, is the replication.

K562 (discovery) RPE1 (replication)
Cells measured 310,385 247,914
Candidate perturbations 1,117 631
Train / validation / test 558 / 279 / 280 315 / 158 / 157
Test hits at \(k\) = 5 % (defined below) 14 8

The task is to predict the mean effect of knocking down a gene never seen in training, as a vector \(\Delta_p\) of log-expression changes over 2,000 genes. The knocked-down gene itself is removed from every score, so that scores reflect the downstream response and not the efficiency of the knockdown.

The predictors are deliberately simple, because the point is to compare evaluation rules on models whose behaviour can be understood. Two constant baselines, control (no effect) and mean (the average training effect), cannot tell candidates apart. Four predictors, two learners times two representations, map a 64-dimensional representation of the target gene to its effect, with either ridge regression or nearest neighbours (the mean effect of the 20 most similar training targets):

  • PCA, learned from the responses: each gene is embedded by how its expression moves across the training perturbations, a signature that even a never-targeted gene has.
  • STRING, from prior knowledge: a spectral embedding of the STRING protein-association graph (Szklarczyk et al. 2023), restricted to its experimental and database channels; the co-expression and text-mining channels are dropped because they could encode Perturb-seq results.

The usual metrics are five: Pearson correlation and MSE on raw expression, Pearson correlation on the effect \(\Delta\), MSE on the 50 genes the perturbation changes most, and a discrimination score (can the prediction identify which perturbation produced a profile?).

The decision metric is hit recall at \(k\). The hits are the \(k\) candidates with the largest transcriptional effect. A model ranks candidates by the size of its predicted effect, \(\lVert\tilde\Delta_p\rVert^2\), and its hit recall is the share of true hits in its top \(k\). At \(k\) = 5 % of the 280 K562 test candidates, there are 14 hits, and a random choice finds 0.7 of them on average.

NoteMeasuring “strongest” without the noise bias

The natural measure of a perturbation’s strength is \(\lVert\Delta_p\rVert^2\), but only a noisy estimate \(\hat\Delta_p = \Delta_p + \varepsilon_p\) is observed, and

\[ \mathbb{E}\,\lVert\hat\Delta_p\rVert^2 = \lVert\Delta_p\rVert^2 + \operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_p). \]

The noise term is larger for perturbations measured on fewer cells, so ranking by the naive norm would push poorly measured perturbations up. The study instead splits the sequencing batches (GEM groups, each with its own control cells) into two disjoint units \(A\) and \(B\) and uses the cross-fitted amplitude

\[ \mathrm{amp}(p) = \big\langle \hat\Delta_p^A, \hat\Delta_p^B \big\rangle, \]

whose expectation is \(\lVert\Delta_p\rVert^2\) when the two errors are independent and centred. The controls must be split too: if both units shared the same control cells, the bias would come back through the shared control noise. After log-normalisation, the unbiasedness is only approximate; on the pooled data the naive and cross-fitted rankings agree closely (Spearman 0.98), and the correction matters mostly at the margin. It removes the average bias of noisy perturbations, not the uncertainty of the ranking itself (Section 3.6).

The protocol. I fixed the plan before looking at K562, and wrote down my predictions for RPE1 before downloading it (plan).

On K562, the usual metrics pick a model that barely beats chance

The five usual metrics agree with each other. On validation, they all choose knn-STRING, which is also the best predictor on every usual test metric. On test, it puts 1 of the 14 hits in its top 14, against 0.7 expected by chance. Hit recall on validation chooses ridge-PCA instead, which finds 5 of 14, the best test value of all predictors (Figure 1). Choosing with hit recall would have found 4 more hits.

The STRING predictors are better at predicting the effects and at recognising perturbations; the PCA predictors are far better at finding the strongest ones. One reason the metrics can disagree with the decision is theoretical: a correlation ignores scale, \(\operatorname{corr}(a x, y) = \operatorname{corr}(x, y)\) for \(a > 0\), while the decision ranks candidates by the size of the predicted effect. A model can get every response’s direction right and its size wrong, and still score well on Pearson on \(\Delta\). That cannot be the whole story here, since the MSE metrics, which do see scale, chose the same predictor.

The raw-expression metrics are worse still. Every predictor, including the one that predicts no effect at all, scores between 0.992 and 0.994 in Pearson correlation on expression. Most genes keep their level after a knockdown, so getting the baseline transcriptome right is enough for a near-perfect score.

Is the four-hit gap robust? The direction is, by a rule I set after seeing the K562 result, so this check is post hoc on K562. Over 50 further random splits of the perturbations, with everything refitted and reselected each time, hit recall always picks a PCA predictor, the usual metrics always pick a STRING predictor, and the gap is positive in 92 % of splits, about 2 hits out of 14 on average (Figure 3, left panel). But on the main split, the half-sampling sensitivity range of the gap reaches zero, so it does not meet the criterion I had frozen in advance.

NoteWhat the sensitivity ranges are, and are not

The uncertainty of the model choice is estimated by half-sampling: 1,000 times, half of the validation perturbations are drawn and every choice is redone, and half of the test perturbations are drawn independently, with the hits redefined within that half. At k = 5 % on K562, each half holds about 7 hits instead of 14. The resulting 2.5–97.5 % ranges describe the variability of a smaller, harder selection problem; they are sensitivity ranges, not guaranteed confidence intervals, and they ignore both the noise of the training set and the noise in the hits themselves. The 50 repeated splits reuse the same perturbations, so they are a robustness check, not independent replications.

The twist: ribosomal proteins

The analyses in this section were added after the first K562 results, prompted by an adversarial review; they are exploratory. When a result looks this clean, it is worth asking which hits the winning model finds. Here the answer is striking: all 5 hits of ridge-PCA target ribosomal proteins (the RPL and RPS genes, which encode parts of the ribosome, the cell’s protein factory). Five of the 14 test hits are ribosomal-protein knockdowns, out of 15 ribosomal-protein candidates in the test set, and ridge-PCA’s top 14 holds 13 of them.

Knocking down a ribosomal protein can disturb many processes at once and produce a large transcriptional response; a biologist would call these hits expected. And a model is not needed to find them. Ranking the test targets by a single number known before any experiment, their baseline expression in control cells, also finds 5 of the 14 hits, as many as ridge-PCA (the green bar in Figure 1). Ribosomal proteins are among the most highly expressed genes in the cell, which likely explains much of the match. I call such a model-free ranking a prior, in the everyday sense of knowledge available before the experiment, not a Bayesian prior.

Remove the ribosomal-protein targets, and the picture changes (Figure 2). At k = 5 %, no advantage over chance is detected: ridge-PCA is numerically above chance (2 hits, 0.143 against 0.053) but not significantly (Monte-Carlo p = 0.16), the other models find none, and the gap between selection rules is zero: hit recall now picks knn-PCA and the usual metrics a STRING predictor, and none of them finds a hit. The predictors do rise above chance for \(k \geq\) 10–15 %, so there is some signal in them, but not at the top of the ranking, which is where a lab with a small budget operates.

So the K562 result says something narrower than it first seemed. The usual metrics and hit recall do disagree, and the disagreement costs hits. But the rule that finds more hits does so, in large part, by exploiting a family of genes that a trivial prior already ranks first. The added value of the model for this decision is not shown.

RPE1: the gap does not replicate

The RPE1 predictions were written down before its data were downloaded. The first, that the usual metrics and hit recall would again disagree, failed. On the main split, all five usual metrics and hit recall choose the same predictor, knn-STRING, and the gap is zero. Over 50 re-splits the mean gap is −0.02, positive in only 18–20 % of splits and negative in 26–30 % (Figure 3, right panel).

What did replicate is the weaker, more useful finding: the target-expression prior again matches or beats the selected model. It finds 2 of the 8 test hits, while knn-STRING, the choice of every metric, finds 1.6 (a fractional count from tie-aware expected recall). One predictor, ridge-STRING, is above chance on RPE1 (3 of 8, one-sided p = 0.005, uncorrected for the four predictors), though no selection rule picked it on validation: a small point in favour of injected biological knowledge.

Why the difference between the two lines? One observation stands out (Figure 4). A perturbation is a candidate only if it has at least 20 cells in each of four batch folds. On K562, 62 of the 78 ribosomal-protein knockdowns pass that filter; on RPE1, only 6 do, and a single one lands in the test set. A plausible reading is that knocking down a ribosomal protein harms RPE1 cells more, so fewer of them are observed, and the quality filter removes almost all of the family that drove the K562 result. Hits on RPE1 also have fewer cells than non-hits (medians 110 against 154), the reverse of K562. This is a post hoc hypothesis: I did not test whether the filter causes the change of verdict, and fewer cells do not prove cell death.

The general point outlives the specific mechanism. When inclusion in a benchmark depends on the biology of the perturbation, the benchmark is not a sample of the interventions one would like to choose among. Filtering and cell survival are part of the learning problem.

The hits themselves are noisy

One last measurement puts all the gaps above in perspective. Splitting the cells of each perturbation into two fully independent halves and computing the top 5 % on each, the two hit lists overlap by only 79 % on K562 and 75 % on RPE1, a conservative estimate since each list uses half of the data. About one hit in four or five changes between two measurements of the same experiment. The differences between models discussed above, a few hits out of 14, are of the same order. The two quantities are not directly comparable, so this does not show that the gap is noise, but it calls for caution.

The same holds for the effect profiles: two independent halves of the same experiment correlate at only 0.48 on \(\Delta\) on K562 (0.55 on RPE1). The best model reaches 0.37. That reference is pessimistic, since each half uses only part of the cells, but it shows how noisy the targets themselves are. It is a yardstick, not an upper bound: a model that estimated the true effect well could beat it.

Noise also bites on the selection side. On RPE1, with about 8 hits in validation, choosing a model by hit recall partly follows noise: across 50 splits, it picks each of the four learners between 14 % and 36 % of the time, and it loses a little on average. A metric aligned with the decision is not enough; it must also be stable enough to select with.

What the study says, and what it does not

It says that, on these two screens and with these simple predictors:

  • the usual metrics agree with each other, and on K562 they choose a predictor that is close to chance at picking the strongest perturbations;
  • a decision metric can choose differently, but its advantage on K562 is matched by a one-number prior, and it vanishes on RPE1;
  • the hits and the choice of model are both uncertain enough that a benchmark should measure their stability, not assume it.

It does not say that hit recall is a better metric in general, that deep or foundation models would behave the same way, or that a large transcriptional effect is the right definition of an interesting perturbation. It is two cell lines from one study, essential genes only, small hit sets, and effectively two families of predictors.

The practical recommendations are modest. A perturbation benchmark that is meant to guide experiments should report a decision metric at the \(k\) matching the experimental budget, compare models against priors that need no model (target expression, membership of known essential programmes), and not only against chance, size its validation set so that model selection is stable, and track which perturbations its filters drop.

The report has the full methods, every pre-specified criterion with its verdict, and the leakage precautions. The code reproduces every result in the report’s tables and figures from the public data.

Will scale fix this?

The study is small, but it sharpens the question I started from. Sutton’s “bitter lesson” says that general methods exploiting computation eventually beat methods built on human knowledge (Sutton 2019). It has been borne out in language, in protein structure (Jumper et al. 2021), in weather forecasting (Lam et al. 2023). Why not, so far, in single cell?

My answer is that the bitter lesson carries an assumption that is easy to forget: the signal must already be in the data. I find it useful to look at the fields where scaling succeeded through five conditions. This is my own grid, not a theorem: none of the conditions is strictly necessary, and the study above tests none of them directly.

1. The pretraining objective is the task, or close to it. Predicting the next token already is generating text. A weather model predicts the state at \(t\) + 6 h from the state at \(t\), with forty years of reanalysis (past observations assimilated into a consistent physical record) providing free input–output pairs. AlphaFold is supervised directly on structures. Single-cell foundation models, by contrast, are pretrained to reconstruct masked genes in observational atlases, while the task that matters is interventional: what happens if I perturb this gene? Learning \(p(X)\) on resting cells does not identify causal effects. Atlases are the climatology of the cell, and climatology is not forecasting. Observational pretraining may still make learning from interventional data more efficient; how much is an open question.

2. The effective data size is large, not just the raw volume. We count cells in the hundreds of millions, but the cells of one condition are replicates of each other. For perturbation prediction the statistical unit is the (perturbation, context) pair. In this study, after the eligibility filters (Figure 4), the real \(n\) was 1,117 on K562 and 631 on RPE1, in two essential-gene screens and exactly two contexts.

3. The target is measured well above the noise. Experimental protein structures are precise relative to the structural differences a predictor must capture. In single-cell RNA sequencing, each measurement samples a small fraction of a cell’s transcripts, with batch effects on top. The study gives an idea of the noise: independent halves of the same experiment agree at 0.48 on the effect profile, and their hit lists overlap by about three quarters. When the targets are this noisy and a mean predictor is not far behind the best models, small differences between models are hard to resolve. I suspect this explains part of why deep models did not beat linear baselines (Ahlmann-Eltze et al. 2025).

4. A blind benchmark against a strong baseline. Protein structure prediction was judged in CASP, a blind competition; weather models are compared on WeatherBench against the operational physics-based model of the European weather centre (ECMWF), a formidable baseline. Progress there was visible and hard to game. In single cell, raw-expression metrics saturate around 0.99 for a model that predicts nothing, and the first Virtual Cell Challenge (Roohani et al. 2025) ended in a debate on metrics that a mean prediction can satisfy (Arc Institute 2025; Chu and Stevens 2025). The study adds one more failure mode: the usual metrics can choose a model that does no better than a single number known before any experiment. Without a good benchmark, real progress would go unseen if it happened.

5. Structure to exploit, and contexts to learn from. The physics of the atmosphere is the same everywhere, on a sphere with known symmetries. Proteins have 3D geometry, hence equivariant architectures. Language is compositional. I know of no structure in the cell as exploitable as the 3D geometry of proteins. And the cell changes with type, donor, protocol and state, while the data cover very few contexts: the questions asked are extrapolative, an unseen perturbation, often in an unseen context, learned from a handful of observed ones. The RPE1 replication is a small illustration: a conclusion drawn on one cell line did not hold on the next, where even the candidate population differed.

Two caveats keep this from being a story of scale versus no scale. Time-series forecasting, the closest analogue to single cell with its heterogeneous series and no shared semantics, is not a clear success of scale: a linear model beat the transformers of the time (Zeng et al. 2023), and today’s foundation models (Ansari et al. 2024) bring convenient zero-shot forecasts; in my reading, their gains over well-tuned statistical baselines are modest. And AlphaFold is not a victory of raw scale either: it relies on a heavily domain-informed architecture and fifty years of the PDB, and its successes are concentrated where those data are dense; dynamics and the effects of single mutations remain much harder.

In the fields above, simple methods won until the condition on the data was met. The current finding in single cell does not show that scale will fail; it suggests that several of these conditions are not yet met. Which of them can be?

What plays for. Biology can generate its data on demand. New protein structures are slow and costly to determine; a lab can run a new Perturb-seq screen relatively quickly, and a closed loop of model and experiment is an advantage the PDB never had. Interventional datasets are growing quickly, and what matters is their diversity of contexts more than their number of cells. And prior knowledge may help: on RPE1, the only predictor above chance used a protein-association graph, though on K562 the same predictors were near chance at the top.

What plays against. Measurement noise limits what any model can show: more cells per condition, more molecules per cell and other modalities would reduce it more surely than a bigger network. The space of perturbations, contexts, doses and times is combinatorially huge, and data cover little of it. And evaluations find that single-cell foundation models often do no better than simple baselines on cell-type annotation, clustering and integration, fine-tuned (Boiarsky et al. 2023) or zero-shot (Kedzierska et al. 2025), and that more observational pretraining does not reliably help, consistent with the first condition: more climatology does not give forecasts.

So I do not expect a generalist virtual cell from scaling up atlases. I expect narrower successes: transfer of perturbation effects between close contexts in heavily measured systems, trained on diverse interventional data, judged with decision-relevant metrics against strong priors, and fed by experiments in a loop. The likely path looks more like weather, with input–output pairs in bulk plus knowledge of the system, than like language models.

Before betting on scale for a given problem, I would now ask three questions:

  1. How reproducible is the measured effect between independent replicates?
  2. How many independent conditions, not cells, are there?
  3. Does the benchmark show progress against a strong baseline, such as the expression level of the targeted gene?

All three can be answered on public screens before building a new model.
